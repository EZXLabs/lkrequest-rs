# HTTP/2 client performance results — 2026-09-05

> **Superseded diagnostic baseline:** these numbers were collected before
> lkrequest enabled `TCP_NODELAY` by default. They remain useful as the failure
> reproduction, but they are not the corrected performance comparison. See
> `DIAGNOSIS-2026-09-05.md` for the root cause and post-fix results.

This report records a local performance comparison of `lkrequest`, Hyper, and
reqwest. It intentionally excludes TLS and wire-fingerprint fidelity. The
benchmark uses one reused h2c connection per client process and fully consumes
every response body.

## Environment

- OS: Windows NT 10.0.26200.0, x86_64
- CPU: Intel64 Family 6 Model 183 Stepping 1, 28 logical processors
- Rust: `rustc 1.100.0-nightly (2026-08-23)`
- Cargo: `cargo 1.100.0-nightly (2026-08-22)`
- lkrequest: `0.2.1`
- Hyper: `1.11.1`
- reqwest: `0.13.4`
- Tokio: `1.49.0`
- Primary runtime configuration: one client worker, two server workers
- Samples: five alternating-order rounds per scenario and client
- Result statistic: median of the five round-level results

## Primary results

### Small response workloads

| Scenario | Client | Throughput (req/s) | p99 (us) | Process CPU (s) | Peak RSS (MiB) |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sequential reuse | lkrequest | 18,937.8 | 111 | 0.812 | 8.8 |
| Sequential reuse | Hyper | 28,618.4 | 77 | 0.484 | 7.7 |
| Sequential reuse | reqwest | 24,170.2 | 100 | 0.578 | 8.3 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 8 | lkrequest | 14,107.7 | 13,771 | 0.844 | 9.1 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 8 | Hyper | 83,938.8 | 219 | 0.266 | 8.1 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 8 | reqwest | 71,234.9 | 255 | 0.344 | 8.6 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 32 | lkrequest | 10,441.3 | 18,323 | 1.734 | 10.0 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 32 | Hyper | 166,534.4 | 485 | 0.281 | 9.0 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 32 | reqwest | 111,173.8 | 574 | 0.438 | 9.6 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 128 | lkrequest | 18,576.9 | 25,142 | 1.453 | 11.7 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 128 | Hyper | 156,575.4 | 1,270 | 0.297 | 10.4 |
| Multiplex, concurrency 128 | reqwest | 120,997.6 | 1,423 | 0.406 | 11.5 |

Relative to lkrequest, Hyper delivered 1.51x sequential throughput, 5.95x at
concurrency 8, 15.95x at concurrency 32, and 8.43x at concurrency 128.
reqwest delivered 1.28x, 5.05x, 10.65x, and 6.51x respectively.

The largest lkrequest regression is therefore not connection setup. Every run
observed exactly one HTTP/2 connection. It appears when many small responses
are dispatched over the multiplexed connection, where lkrequest tail latency
rises into tens of milliseconds while Hyper and reqwest remain below roughly
1.5 milliseconds in this matrix.

### One MiB response, concurrency 32

| Client | Throughput (MiB/s) | p99 (us) | Process CPU (s) | Peak RSS (MiB) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| lkrequest | 901.9 | 72,539 | 0.766 | 30.4 |
| Hyper | 781.2 | 89,828 | 0.688 | 20.6 |
| reqwest | 760.6 | 106,144 | 0.734 | 20.8 |

lkrequest led large-body throughput by approximately 15.5% over Hyper and
18.6% over reqwest, with lower p99 latency. Its peak RSS was approximately 47%
higher than either comparison client.

## Runtime sensitivity

Two additional five-round matrices checked whether the primary single-worker
runtime unfairly constrained a client:

- Four client workers, two server workers: lkrequest reached 20,444 req/s at
  concurrency 32, 28,875 req/s at concurrency 128, and 1,182 MiB/s for the
  one-MiB workload. Hyper and reqwest still reached 123,000–160,000 req/s for
  the concurrency 32/128 small-body workloads.
- Four client workers, four server workers: lkrequest reached 18,864 req/s at
  concurrency 32, 27,096 req/s at concurrency 128, and 1,424 MiB/s for the
  one-MiB workload. Hyper and reqwest still reached 89,807–130,747 req/s and
  95,161–125,764 req/s respectively for the small-body workloads.
- All clients suffered a roughly 15 ms tail-latency scheduling cliff in the
  concurrency-8 multi-worker runs on this Windows host. These matrices are
  sensitivity evidence, not headline rankings.

## Conclusions

1. lkrequest is moderately slower for sequential reused small requests, but
   the much larger performance gap begins in multiplexed small-response work.
2. The connection-count assertion rules out accidental reconnecting as the
   explanation. Profiling should focus on the native H2 driver, response
   dispatch, wakeups, and body-delivery path before changing protocol behavior.
3. lkrequest is competitive and fastest in this test for large fully-buffered
   bodies, at the cost of materially higher peak memory.
4. Hyper is the fastest small-response baseline in the primary matrix, while
   reqwest adds modest high-level overhead over Hyper and still remains much
   faster than lkrequest under multiplexing.

## Boundaries

- This is an h2c prior-knowledge benchmark. It excludes DNS, TCP connect, TLS,
  certificate validation, proxies, compression, redirects, cookies, and all
  fingerprint-fidelity checks.
- The server also uses Hyper. That may favor the Hyper/h2 ecosystem, and a
  neutral `nghttp2` server cross-check was not available on this machine.
- Results came from a Windows development workstation, not an isolated Linux
  performance host. Round-level variance was substantial in several highly
  concurrent and large-body scenarios, so medians should be treated as
  directional engineering evidence rather than a public leaderboard.
- The comparison covers mainstream Rust clients with equivalent explicit h2c
  prior-knowledge support. TLS-only or differently architected clients should
  be compared in a separate TLS benchmark matrix.
